Sorting a House

How to help a family decide what stays and what goes, without the day going wrong. Written for someone doing it for the first time.

For The concierge running the sorting days In the app Job Plan, In the house, under the boxes Status Draft for Anthony Issued 17 Oct 2026

Why the order matters

Families that start with the photographs stall. Families that build momentum through the easy rooms finish. So the house is sorted from the least emotional room to the most, whatever order it was walked in for the estimate.

  1. Garage, utility and storage. Tools, paint, boxes nobody has opened in years. Fast decisions, visible progress.
  2. Guest rooms. Furniture the client does not sleep in and linen nobody uses.
  3. Kitchen. Duplicates, expired food, gadgets. Keep the set they cook with.
  4. Office and papers. See Papers below: there is always more than it looks.
  5. Bedrooms and closets. Clothes are personal but not sentimental for most people.
  6. Living and dining rooms. The big pieces, and the first real conversations about the new home.
  7. Sentimental things last. Photographs, letters, heirlooms, holiday decorations. By now the family has practice.

If the plan's rooms are in a different order, that is the order the house was walked for the estimate. Work them in the order above anyway.

Rule 1

Two to three hours, once a day

Decisions tire people out. Tired people keep everything, or panic and throw out what they will miss, and long sessions end in arguments. Stop while it is still going well.

Rule 2

The client decides first

The family advises. Nobody claims a piece before the client has said what happens to it, and nothing is cleared out as a surprise. Ever.

Rule 3

Sort by use and space, not price

Never debate what something is worth standing in the room. Valuing is a separate job, done on the computer afterwards and by specialists. Press the Appraise flag when you shoot it and move on.

Four decisions for everything

Every object gets one of four answers, and the destinations you tap in the camera are those answers: Keep (or New home on a move), To a person, Sell or Donate, and Remove.

  • Undecided is allowed, with a date. A "not sure" pile is fine when the family agrees on the day they will come back to it. It is not a fifth answer that lives in the garage until move day.
  • Gifts to family have a claim date. "Anything you want, tell us by Friday." After that, what is unclaimed goes to sale or donation. Say the date on the pre-job call and again on the first sorting day.
  • Quick wins first in every room. Expired products, broken things, duplicates (keep the best one, donate the rest). Ten minutes of this gets a room moving.
  • The one-year test for every Keep. Not used or loved in the past year? It almost certainly belongs in another pile. Ask the question gently and let the client answer it.

Keep the memory, not the volume

Most sentimental things are kept for the memory, not the object. Photograph the piece before it goes, with the client holding it if they like, and the memory lives in the picture. A single curated memory box, a labelled album or a family journal does what six boxes of loose keepsakes cannot.

Where a piece really is going to a family member, record who it goes to in the camera (To a person) so the record says so, and it leaves the house with its name on it.

Papers

  • Paper is always more than it looks. Plan a session for it on its own.
  • Financial records older than seven years can usually go to the shredder. Tax returns, deeds, titles, insurance policies, wills and trust papers, military records and anything with a court on it stay, and on an estate they go to the representative or the attorney against a signed receipt.
  • Digitise what matters (photographs, letters the family wants to keep) rather than boxing it.
  • What travels with the client is set aside now: the documents they will need in the first week at the new home. They go in the to-go bag, never on the truck (Move Day).
  • Shredding is a vendor's job: the Job Plan's Midpoint & pickups stage has the box for it when a shredding vendor is on the job.

Donations are not disposal

Worn, broken or stained is not a donation. Dropping it at a charity passes the problem on to volunteers, and the receipt is worth nothing. Dispose of it honestly and donate what somebody can use. Gently used furniture and household goods go to the furniture charities; many collect for free. Book the pickups as the piles grow, not the week of the move.

Safety while you sort

  • Pathways clear, boxes out of walkways, exits never blocked. Piles grow faster than anyone expects.
  • Nothing stacked on the stairs. Nothing heavy on a high shelf because it was "going anyway".
  • Anything that worries you, from a hazard in the house to a person in it, goes on the Incidents card the same day.

The sorting day

  1. Easy rooms first, sentimental last.
  2. Two to three hours. Then stop.
  3. The client decides, the family advises.
  4. No claiming, no surprises.
  5. Quick wins first: expired, broken, duplicates.
  6. Not used in a year? Another pile.
  7. Undecided needs a date.
  8. Photograph it before it goes.
  9. Never price it in the room. Flag it.
  10. Broken is not a donation.
